# LM Boards — Connectedness & Positive Psychology (Section 10)

## Objective 1 — Positive psychology in behavior-change counseling

- Positive psychology = the study of what allows individuals and
  communities to flourish; strengths-based, amplifies well-being via
  positive affect, engagement, meaning — doesn't disregard negative
  states [refs 1–3]. Underpins **positive health** (well-being beyond
  absence of disease) [ref 4].
- **PERMA (Seligman)** [refs 5–9]: **P**ositive emotion,
  **E**ngagement (Csikszentmihalyi "flow"), **R**elationships,
  **M**eaning, **A**ccomplishment.
- **Character strengths** [ref 10]: expression raises engagement and
  positive emotion; only ~1/3 of adults know their strengths; six
  virtue categories — wisdom/knowledge, courage, humanity, justice,
  temperance, transcendence; free VIA inventory
  (viacharacter.org).
- **PPIs** (positive psychology interventions) = treatment methods/
  intentional activities cultivating positive feelings, behaviors,
  or cognition [ref 11]; complementary to LM. **Lyubomirsky set-point
  theory** [ref 12]: happiness = genetics + circumstances +
  intentional activities (the modifiable slice).
- **Fredrickson broaden-and-build** [ref 13]: positive emotions
  broaden thought and build social/physical/intellectual/artistic
  resources. Ten most common positive emotions: joy, gratitude,
  serenity, interest, hope, pride, amusement, inspiration, awe,
  love. Optimism ↔ up to 15% longer life, survival advantage past 85
  [refs 14–17]. PPIs can target past (forgiveness, gratitude),
  present (savoring, contentment), future (hope, optimism).

## Objective 2 — Positive psychology for sustaining healthy behaviors

- Framework for behavior change: upward cycle of optimism, effort,
  efficacy, learning. Providers keep screening/diagnosing/referring
  AND prescribe PPIs + leverage positive psychology in conversations
  (e.g., "What was the best experience you had managing diabetes
  this month? … What did you learn?").
- Habit menu [refs 6, 18–22]: find/improve signature strengths;
  count blessings; express appreciation; savor; write how you want
  to be remembered; regular acts of kindness; recall happiest days;
  meaningful activities; connect in person (not mainly social
  media).
- In chronic disease: PPI practice increases positive emotions and
  well-being, lowers distressing thoughts and SI risk [ref 17];
  positive psychological assets prospectively associate with better
  health, but **not yet shown to treat chronic illness alone**
  [ref 23]; PPI effects on well-being/depression are **small but
  significant** (meta-analyses) [ref 24].

## Objective 3 — Eudaimonia vs hedonia; purpose and longevity

- **Eudaimonia** (Deci & Ryan): living well/actualizing one's
  potentials — a process of fulfilling one's "daimon"/true nature;
  meaningful goal-directedness [refs 25–26]. **Hedonia:** maximizing
  happiness — presence of positive emotions, absence of negative,
  satisfaction of desires.
- **Purpose in life evidence (exam favorites):** strong purpose →
  mortality HR **0.60** in community-dwelling elderly; less
  Alzheimer's/MCI and slower cognitive decline [ref 27]; Health and
  Retirement Study — higher purpose → fewer strokes and MIs, more
  preventive labs/screening [refs 28–29]; chronic disease + purpose/
  positive relationships/positive affect → lower IL-6 and CRP
  [ref 30]; **gene regulation:** eudaimonia downregulates
  pro-inflammatory genes and upregulates antibody-synthesis genes;
  hedonia shows the opposite pattern [refs 31–32]; purpose → faster
  emotional recovery from negative events [refs 13, 33]; eudaimonic
  well-being ↔ greater insular-cortex gray matter [ref 34].

## Objective 4 — Social connectedness, networks, and longevity

- Social connectedness = relationships where people feel **seen,
  heard, valued**, drawing sustenance and strength [ref 35].
- **Harvard Study of Adult Development** (75-yr cohort; 268 Harvard
  + 456 inner-city Boston men): the single most important predictor
  of happiness AND longevity = **social connections** [refs 36–37].
- **Network contagion** (Framingham network analyses): emotional
  states spread socially up to **three degrees of separation**
  (friends of friends' friends), influencing health behaviors
  (happiness, obesity spread) [refs 38–39].
- Social isolation: neurologically resembles **physical pain**
  (shared neural representations) [ref 40].
- **Positivity resonance** [refs 41–42]: social interactions with
  shared positivity, mutual care, concern; **micro-moments of
  connection** (brief authentic interactions, any context) improve
  longevity, health, physiologic measures (heart rate).

## Objective 5 — Social media: harms and benefits

- >4.6B users (more than half the planet) [ref 43].
- Young adults 19–32: highest vs lowest social-media-use quartile →
  depression **aOR 2.74** (dose-dependent, linear) [ref 44]; 44% of
  US young adults report problematic social media use (PSMU) —
  strongly, independently associated with depressive symptoms
  [ref 45]; adolescents: use ↔ poor sleep, anxiety, depression, low
  self-esteem — poor sleep tracks **nighttime-specific** use;
  anxiety/depression track **emotional investment** in sites
  [ref 46].
- Mechanisms proposed [refs 47–50]: displacement theory (online time
  displaces healthy behaviors); reinforcing-spirals theory (curated
  feeds → confirmation bias → declining well-being); increased
  negative internalizing/rumination. Evidence base still thin;
  benefits and harms can coexist — user intention/actions shape
  affective outcomes [ref 48].

## Objective 6 — Lifestyle pillars × positive emotions × flourishing

- **Therapeutic lifestyle change (TLC)** can equal psychotherapy and
  pharmacotherapy for promoting mental health [refs 51–53];
  components: exercise, nutrition, time in nature, supportive
  relationships, recreation/relaxation/stress management,
  religious/spiritual involvement, service to others, sleep
  modification, mindfulness, avoiding risky substances. Diet, sleep,
  and physical activity are especially important for major
  depression and anxiety [refs 54–55].
- Exercise: small positive effect vs no treatment for depression;
  **not superior** to psychotherapy/pharmacotherapy; as effective as
  pharmacotherapy for mild-moderate depression; synergy with
  antidepressants; effective in the elderly; childhood activity
  protective into young adulthood [refs 56–62].
- Sleep: UK student digital-CBT trial (n=3,755, 26 universities) —
  improved sleep reduced insomnia, paranoia, hallucinations at 10
  wk [ref 63]; ~100k Japanese young adults — 7–9 h = best mental
  health, linear sleep-quality relationship [ref 64].
- Meditation: mild-moderate reductions in negative dimensions of
  stress [ref 65]. Excess alcohol: HTN, CVD, stroke, liver disease,
  cancers, immunity, learning/memory, depression/anxiety, social
  harms [ref 66]. Nutrition-mood: see Sections 5 and 7.

## Objective 7 — PPI activities to prescribe

- Signature strengths (VIA inventory, use one new way); count
  blessings (3 good things nightly); express appreciation ("what
  were your wins this week?"); savoring (5 min/day on one activity);
  legacy exercise ("50 years from now, I want people to remember me
  for…"); acts of kindness (note the feeling afterward); happiest-
  days recall (photo scrolling); meaningful activities (reflect on
  purpose served); in-person connection (family, social, sport,
  spiritual groups) [refs 6, 20–22].
- Mindfulness: associated with hedonic and eudaimonic well-being
  gains; field needs uniform definition and rigor [refs 67–68];
  mildly lowers stress even with mental-health disorders [ref 69];
  college students — lower stress, less avoidance, more assertive
  coping [ref 70].
- Promotion tips: all LM elements potentiate emotional well-being;
  **prescribe dosed TLC**; celebrate successes and link emotional
  boosts to physical change; prioritize practitioner self-care —
  it drives clinician "presence" with patients [ref 71].
