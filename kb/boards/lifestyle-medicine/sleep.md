# LM Boards — Sleep Health (Section 8)

## Objective 1 — Sleep's role in health and disease

- Sleep = duration + timing + quality; ask about all three.
- **Statistics** [refs 1–4]: average US adult sleeps 6 h 57 min; 20%
  <6 h; 37% of young adults <7 h (doubled 1960→2002); 60M US adults
  with frequent sleep difficulty, only 10% seek care; insomnia
  indirect costs ~$60B/yr (presenteeism, disability, errors); a sleep
  disorder raises mortality risk ~1.5× after adjustment [ref 5].
- **Sleep needs** (Table 8.1 [ref 6]): adults 18+ — **7–9 h**;
  teens 13–18 — 9–10 h; 5–12 y — 10–11 h; 3–5 y — 11–13 h; quick
  guideposts: newborn 16 h, 2 y 12.5 h, 10 y 10.5 h, teen 9.5 h,
  adult 8.5 h. No objective test of individual need — sleep-diary
  titration: extend by 15 min every 3 d–1 wk until the sleep period
  is no longer fully filled [ref 7]. Deficiency impairs performance
  with poor self-awareness of impairment.
- 14.5% of workers work evening/night/alternative shifts; 26.5% of
  them have **shift work sleep disorder** [refs 8–9]; average
  full-time work hours 2021: 54 h/wk [ref 10].
- **Circadian physiology** [refs 11–20]: master clock runs ~24.1–24.4
  h (easier to delay than advance); **SCN** in hypothalamus =
  oscillator + pacemaker; entrained by **zeitgebers**, light the most
  potent — brightness (melanopsin retinal ganglion cells), not
  images. **Phase response curve:** midday light pulse — no shift;
  early subjective night — ~30-min delay (device at night); later
  pre-midnight — ~60-min delay (bathroom light flip); mid-night —
  ~60-min advance; end of subjective night — ~30-min **advance**
  (healthy: wake, bright bathroom, step outside). Peripheral clocks
  in all tissues feed back to the SCN; food and activity entrain
  them.
- **Melatonin** [refs 15–19]: pineal "night hormone," serotonin
  derivative; signals "low light + nighttime," not sleep per se;
  rises ~2 h before expected bedtime, peaks ~30 min before; first
  light stops production ~30 min after waking. Suppression correlates
  with affective disorders; circulating melatonin inhibits SERT →
  GI upset with late-night eating or early forced waking. More
  melatonin-suppression-sensitive: behavioral-health populations
  (bipolar, SAD); aging adults produce less (cataracts, corneal
  opacity).
- **Adenosine = sleep pressure** [ref 20]: builds through wakefulness
  (caffeine antagonizes); insufficient sleepers carry residual
  adenosine → "afternoon dip" sleepiness 2–4 PM vs a normal lull.
- **Disruption harms** [refs 21–34]: healthy sleep → lower cortisol/
  glucose, insulin sensitivity, daytime leptin, lower sympathetic
  tone and BP, nighttime core cooling. Misalignment ("social jet
  lag", shiftwork, DST) → metabolic/autonomic/endocrine dysfunction;
  obesity (2-fold), metabolic syndrome, T2DM, CV risk and mortality,
  refractory HTN and absent nocturnal dipping, MI, vasospasm.
  Cognition/mood: REM loss → worse learning, memory, emotional
  regulation, fear extinction (amygdala); short sleep correlates with
  MDD, bipolar, SAD, PMS, worsened PTSD (deprivation ±2 wk around
  trauma) and TBI outcomes. Cancer [ref 34]: healthy sleep supports
  DNA repair, apoptosis, anti-cancer cytokines (IL-1, IL-2, TNF-α);
  disruption correlates with breast, endometrial, prostate,
  colorectal cancer and AML.

## Objective 2 — Sleep assessment

- **PSG (in-lab sleep study)** [refs 35–36]: order only when the
  differential includes breathing-related disorder (OSA), narcolepsy,
  nocturnal seizures, periodic limb movement disorder, REM sleep
  behavior disorder. **NOT required** for circadian disorders,
  insomnia, NREM partial-arousal parasomnias, nightmare disorder,
  RLS. Gold standard for staging; produces hypnogram.
- **Architecture:** NREM 1 (~2%, entry), NREM 2 (~50%; spindles +
  K-complexes), NREM 3 (20–25%; slow-wave/delta; first half of
  night; physical restoration, immune), REM (20–25%; first episode
  90–120 min after onset; lengthens through night; memory
  consolidation, emotional processing, dreams, atonia). Infants <6
  mo: active/quiet sleep, enter through REM [ref 37].
- **Home sleep studies:** OSA symptom assessment only — no EEG, no
  staging; capture respiration, one cardiac variable, SpO2.
- **Actigraphy:** validated vs PSG; consumer wearables underestimate
  sleep (algorithms track activity) — actigraphs slightly
  overestimate [ref 38].
- **Subjective tools:** sleep diary ≥1 wk (bedtime, waketime,
  latency, wakings, quality); retrospective mini sleep assessment
  (weekday/weekend patterns + perceived quality). Disturbance signs:
  <7 h or >9 h; ≥1 h weekday-weekend difference; irregular timing
  (shiftwork); frequent daytime fatigue/sleepiness.
- **Questionnaires:** PSQI (general, long — not first-line);
  **STOP-BANG** (OSA screen, not central apnea): Snoring, Tired,
  Observed apnea, Pressure (HTN), BMI >35, Age >50, Neck ≥17″ M /
  ≥16″ F, Gender male — 0–2 low, 3–4 intermediate, **5–8 high →
  sleep study**; high-risk also via 2+ STOP items + male, or + BMI
  >35, or + neck size [ref 39]. **Epworth Sleepiness Scale**: 8
  situations, 0–3 dozing likelihood; **≥8 = excessive daytime
  sleepiness** → review for sleep disorders [refs 40–41]. ASHS
  (adolescent sleep-hygiene beliefs/behaviors, 28 items) [ref 42];
  **WHIIRS** (5-item insomnia frequency) [ref 43]; Composite
  Morningness (≤22 eveningness / ≥44 morningness) chronotype.

## Objective 3 — Lifestyle interventions for sleep health

- Assess then address: patterns, environment, daytime/pre-bed
  behaviors [refs 6, 35, 44].
- **Patterns:** insufficient → extend sleep period slowly; excessive
  time in bed → restrict slowly; variable (>1 h weekday-weekend
  difference or >30 min daily variability) → **anchor a consistent
  waketime** and work backward; shift weekend schedules earlier
  15–30 min/wk; duration mismatches → average need across 7 days and
  converge both timing and duration; cultural note — up to ~30–60
  min weekend difference is manageable. Napping (age >7): eliminate;
  if unavoidable, ≤20 min, no later than 2 PM.
- **Environment ("sleep cave": cool, dark, quiet — not silent)**
  [ref 44]: cold extremities → pre-bed bath/shower, socks, less
  late caffeine (vasoconstriction); hot sleepers (reproductive-cycle
  phases, menopause) → fan, cooling mats, cooler room; noise → white
  noise (machine or fan at medium/high), step TV/radio volume down
  nightly; light → remove/dim sources, nightlight out of sight line,
  eye mask, blackout curtains; bright light on waking (natural or
  alarm-clock light); afternoon/evening — table lamps not overhead,
  blinds closed, blue-blocking glasses; **stimulus control: bed =
  sleep and sex only**.
- **Daytime/pre-bed:** bedtime routine starting 15–20 min before
  bed; stop stimulating work 60–90 min out; low-stimulation
  individualized activities; last activity relaxing, minimal light,
  outside bed; worriers → to-do list earlier in evening, relaxation,
  MBSR. Diet [ref 45]: no nighttime caffeine, limit daytime
  caffeine, no alcohol within 3 h, no late snacking, avoid
  high-sodium dinners, hydrate by late afternoon, weight reduction
  if BMI elevated. Exercise [ref 46]: daytime activity, hourly
  movement, morning or late-afternoon/early-evening; individual
  variation allowed; avoid pain-level overexertion.
- **Low-dose melatonin (0.25 mg)** = phase shifter, **not a sleep
  aid** [ref 47]: take 3–4 h before intended bedtime; may repeat
  0.25 mg 30–60 min before bed if not sleepy.
- **Scenario prescriptions** [ref 35]: delayed sleep onset — morning
  sunlight, afternoon outdoor exercise, dim evenings, carb-rich
  breakfast + low-carb dinner, wind-down with active relaxation;
  fragmentation — dark cooling bedroom, bedside water, late-PM
  hydration, no evening diuretics (soda/caffeine/alcohol), address
  rumination; early waking — warm covers in early morning, avoid
  bright light until ideal waketime, late-day sunlight and evening
  activity, 6,500K blue light until 1 h before bed, delay caffeine
  until 30–60 min post-wake, shift carbs from breakfast to dinner.
- **Jet lag** [ref 48]: eastward — bright light (outdoors) near new
  waketime, dim-light hour before new bedtime, hearty breakfast
  within 30–45 min of new waketime, melatonin per above; westward —
  late-afternoon/evening bright light, complex-carb dinner 2–3 h
  before new bedtime, melatonin.

## Objective 4 — Common sleep disorders (risk assessment)

- Most common: children/teens — insomnia, circadian disorders, OSA,
  partial-arousal parasomnias; adults/pregnancy — insomnia, OSA,
  RLS [ref 49]; shift workers — shift work sleep disorder.
- **OSA** [refs 39, 50–52]: prevalence 3.2% (20–44) → 11.3% (45–64)
  → **18.1% (65+)**; pregnancy 1.4–16.9%; Black children 4× risk.
  Apnea/hypopnea ≥10 s; **AHI severity — adults: mild 5–<15,
  moderate 15–<30, severe ≥30; pediatric: mild 2–<5, moderate 5–<10,
  severe ≥10**. Exam: BMI, neck ≥17″ M/≥15″ F (as taught), craniofacial
  anatomy, crowded airway; difficult-to-control HTN, GERD, asthma/
  COPD associations.
- **Insomnia** [refs 53–54]: most common disorder/complaint; children
  20–30%, teens 7–11%, adults 9–11%; pregnancy 12.6–73.5%; female and
  older Latino adults at risk; 2× risk at puberty onset. Diagnosis:
  difficulty initiating/maintaining sleep despite adequate
  opportunity + daytime consequences, **≥3 nights/wk for ≥3 months**.
  Pediatric types: limit-setting, sleep-onset association, combined.
  Short-sleep misperception exists; triggers include medications,
  vascular dysfunction, visual impairment, renin-angiotensin
  dysfunction.
- **Circadian phase disorders** [refs 55–56]: delayed phase 7–16%
  (ages 13–40); advanced phase 1% (65+); shift work disorder 26.5%
  of shift workers. Treat like insomnia + timed light: delayed —
  morning bright light + 0.25 mg melatonin 3–4 h pre-target-bedtime;
  advanced — afternoon/evening bright light, morning low-dose
  melatonin possible.
- **NREM partial-arousal parasomnias** [refs 52, 57–60]: sleep
  terrors peak 34.4% at 18 mo; sleepwalking peak 13.4% at 10 y;
  sexsomnia 11% M/4% F. Complex goal-directed behaviors in first
  third of night (stage 3) vs **nightmares in last third (REM)**; no
  memory of event; triggers = insufficient/irregular sleep +
  stimulus during deep sleep. Treatment: safety, extend sleep,
  protect the sleep environment.
- **Narcolepsy** [refs 61–65]: central hypersomnolence; 0.005%;
  orexin loss; EDS, sleep-wake transitions, hypnagogic/hypnopompic
  hallucinations, sleep paralysis, cataplexy (often absent at
  presentation).
- **Behaviorally induced insufficient sleep syndrome (BIISS)** [refs
  53, 66–67]: 7.3–20%; voluntary restriction, weekend catch-up; ages
  30–39, alcohol abuse, >40 h work weeks, stress, depression.
- **RLS** [refs 68–69]: 3.9–14.3%; pregnancy 26–32%; urge to move
  legs with rest/evening worsening not better explained otherwise;
  85% have periodic movements of sleep; **iron abnormalities —
  ferritin <75 ng/mL or elevated**; magnesium link unconfirmed
  [ref 70].
- **Periodic limb movement disorder** [ref 71]: 4–11%, rises after
  50; female, shiftwork, stress, caffeine; ~30-s-interval movements
  patient is unaware of; restless sleep/middle-of-night waking +
  daytime sleepiness; often with RLS.

## Objective 5 — Referral to sleep specialist / sleep study

- Sleep medicine specialist = physician board-certified in sleep
  medicine; most sleep disorders are behaviorally based — behavior
  must be addressed regardless.
- Refer/PSG when triage suggests a PSG-requiring disorder (OSA
  unless you treat it yourself, narcolepsy, PLMD) or possible RLS.
- **OSA treatment:** PAP (usually CPAP) = gold standard; BiPAP if
  CPAP ineffective/not tolerated [ref 36]. **Non-adherence is the
  major impediment**: payer adherence definition **≥4 h/night**;
  long-term CPAP non-adherence ~34% over 20 y → behavioral
  interventions; non-adherent confirmed OSA → behavioral sleep
  medicine specialist [ref 72].
- Narcolepsy and PLMD: diagnostic PSG + specialist medication
  management [ref 73]; RLS not due to iron deficiency → specialist.

## Objective 6 — CBT-I and referral to behavioral sleep medicine

- Behavioral sleep medicine specialist = non-physician clinician
  board-certified in behavioral sleep medicine (evidence-based
  behavioral treatment; also the referral for CPAP non-adherence).
- **Sleep hygiene alone is significantly less effective than CBT
  approaches for insomnia** [ref 74].
- **CBT-I = first-line for chronic insomnia, over medications**
  [ref 44]: more sustained benefit, no tolerance/side-effect risk.
  Components [refs 75–81]:
  - **Stimulus control therapy** — most effective single treatment:
    bed for sleep/sex only; if awake >15–20 min, get up, low-key
    activity until sleepy, return; repeat.
  - **Sleep restriction therapy:** prescribe bed window ≈ actual
    sleep + buffer, ~20–30 min shorter than current time in bed
    (e.g., sleeping 6 h → allow 5 h 30 min; fixed waketime 6:00 →
    bedtime 12:30, then 12:15, then 12:00 weekly); extend 15–20
    min/wk while sleep quality = sleep time/sleep period ≥90%;
    normal sleep latency 15–20 min is expected awake time.
  - Relaxation training: effective in moderate-high-quality RCTs
    [ref 82]; not standalone-criteria-meeting for adults >60.
  - Cognitive therapy: restructure anxiety-producing/erroneous
    sleep beliefs [ref 77].
- **Hypnotics/sedatives and mortality** [refs 81, 83–84]:
  prescription hypnotics — significant death-rate increase (18–55 y)
  and all-age all-cause mortality (overdose, crashes, falls,
  depression, cancer, suicide); **not recommended first-line**;
  promote addiction via the "crash night" rebound (2–3 bad nights
  after stopping, then a normal third/fourth night reinforces the
  aid — pattern occurs with or without the drug).
