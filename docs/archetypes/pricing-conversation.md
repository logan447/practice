# The Pricing Conversation

How pricing is introduced across the complimentary consultation so that when
the proposal arrives, the patient's reaction is:

> *"That seems fair. I understand exactly what I'm getting, why it costs
> that amount, and I feel good about moving forward."*

— and never surprise, discomfort, or quiet suspicion of being overcharged.

## The design problem, stated honestly

The failure mode is structural, not moral: an unhurried, empathetic
consultation builds trust and connection — and then a price named for the
first time at the end converts that warmth into an ambush. The patient
either feels sold to, or accepts while privately uncertain. Both outcomes
poison a relationship whose entire premise is candor.

The fix is not softer delivery at the end. It is **making the economics a
normal part of the conversation from the first minute**, so the final
number is an arithmetic conclusion the patient can already see coming.

## Expectations develop in stages, not at the end

**Before the first conversation (website, scheduling page):**
- The pricing *model* is public: how pricing works (time + complexity +
  resources, at a practice rate that exists so the practice can exist),
  and honest reference ranges per archetype-like examples ("ongoing care
  arrangements typically run $X–$Y/month; time-boxed problem-solving
  engagements $A–$B"). Nobody should book the free consultation without
  a rough idea of the eventual number's neighborhood.
- Why publish ranges when pricing is individualized? Because the
  alternative — "contact us for pricing" — is precisely the concierge
  pattern that breeds price anxiety, and because the ranges are true:
  the framework produces them.

**First conversation (mostly listening):**
- Near the close, one plain sentence: *"Before we meet again I'll review
  your records and think about what I could actually do for you. If I
  think I can genuinely help, I'll bring you a written proposal — what
  I'd do, what we'd track, and what it costs, all in one page. If I don't
  think I'm the right answer, I'll tell you that too, and it will have
  cost you nothing."*
- This does three things: names that a price is coming (no ambush),
  frames it inside a document (depersonalizes the money moment), and
  establishes that "no" is an acceptable outcome in both directions.

**Second conversation (the proposal):**
- The proposal is presented **as a whole** — problems, plan, tracking,
  and economics on the same page — so the price arrives already attached
  to what it buys. The price is never the last item discussed after
  everything else is agreed; it is read together with the plan.
- The derivation is offered, not defended: *"The price reflects the
  roughly N hours of my time your situation needs this year. If your
  year turns out lighter, the renewal price drops; if heavier, we
  re-scope together before anything changes."* Then stop talking.
- Explicit invitation to not decide now: *"Take it home. If it doesn't
  feel clearly worth it, that's important information for both of us."*
  A patient who enrolls ambivalently becomes an unhappy patient and an
  unpaid invoice; the slow ramp (D-018) means there is no pressure to
  close.

**At every renewal checkpoint:**
- The same page shows what was planned, what was delivered, what changed
  clinically, and what it cost (D-023). Renewal pricing follows measured
  reality in both directions — this is where the practice *earns* the
  trust the first proposal asked for on faith.

## Principles (testable against every future proposal)

1. **No first-mention-at-the-end.** The price's neighborhood is known
   before the consultation; the mechanism is known before the proposal.
2. **Price and value share a page.** Never present economics detached
   from the plan they fund.
3. **Show the derivation.** Hours, complexity, resources — the D-002
   framework said out loud. Defensible means *actually derived*, not
   confidently asserted.
4. **Both directions.** Lighter-than-expected years get cheaper renewals,
   and the physician says so unprompted. One-directional honesty isn't
   honesty.
5. **Room to say no.** Explicitly offered, sincerely meant, economically
   affordable (slow ramp is a feature here).
6. **The proposal doubles as the legal estimate.** For self-pay patients
   a written Good Faith Estimate is a No Surprises Act requirement (45
   CFR 149.610 — see `payment-landscape.md`), with patient dispute rights
   if final charges exceed it by $400+. Design consequence: the proposal
   *is* the GFE's honest cousin — one number, kept. Re-scoping happens
   at checkpoints in writing, never as a surprise on a statement.
7. **The consultation is free and genuinely diagnostic in both
   directions.** Fit assessment includes economic fit; discovering a
   mismatch politely at proposal stage is a success of the process, not
   a failed sale.
