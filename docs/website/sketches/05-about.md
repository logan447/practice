## Section order and copy

### 1 · Introduction (photo left ~40%; portrait candidate: near the upright piano)

> # About me
> I'm Logan DuBose — a physician and researcher. I've spent my career
> in two places at once: practicing primary care, and leading research
> on why healthcare so often fails the people who need it most. This
> practice is where those two careers meet: one doctor, a small panel,
> and enough time to do medicine properly.

### 2 · Why I practice this way (light band; essay)

> My path isn't the usual one, so let me tell it to you straight. I
> earned my M.D. at the Texas A&M College of Medicine and completed an
> internal medicine internship at George Washington University. Then,
> instead of continuing down the standard hospital track, I made a bet
> on a question that had bothered me through all of training: why does
> a system with this much medicine in it leave so many people feeling
> unseen?
>
> I spent the next years working on that question — leading research
> funded by the National Institutes of Health on aging, caregiving, and
> access to care, and building tools to help families navigate a system
> that seems designed to be unnavigable. The whole time, I kept
> practicing primary care, including as a volunteer physician at a
> charity health center, where I cared for people the system had priced
> out entirely.
>
> Here is what years of studying healthcare's failures taught me: most
> people are not failed by a lack of medicine. They are failed by
> fragmentation — fifteen-minute visits, results that arrive without
> explanation, five doctors and no one holding the whole picture. I
> have measured that problem, published on it, and watched it happen to
> people I cared about.
>
> This practice is my answer. Not a company, not a platform — a return
> to the simplest unit of medicine: one physician and one person, with
> enough time between them. I keep the panel small and the overhead
> near zero so the price can stay at $100 a month and the visits can be
> as long as they need to be. I practice alongside my continuing
> research on healthy aging — each one makes me better at the other.

### 3 · About experience — stated plainly (white; the trust move)

> I believe trust starts with precision about qualifications, so here
> is mine without varnish: I completed medical school and an internal
> medicine internship, and I have practiced primary care part-time for
> years while leading research full-time. I have not spent decades in
> an exam room, and I won't pretend otherwise.
>
> What I promise instead is a way of practicing: I work from evidence.
> I look things up rather than guess. I think before I prescribe. I say
> "I don't know" when I don't — and then I find out. And I refer early
> and without ego, because knowing when a problem needs a specialist,
> an urgent care visit, or an emergency room is one of the most
> important skills in primary care. This practice is built around those
> habits: time to think, time to investigate, and no financial pressure
> to do anything but what your situation needs.

### 5 · How I practice (white; five commitments as testable behaviors)

> **Evidence first.** I recommend what good evidence supports. When the
> evidence is thin or mixed, I say so, and we decide together with that
> honesty on the table.
> **I explain my reasoning.** You will know not just what I recommend
> but why — and what I'd expect to happen if we chose differently.
> **I say "I don't know" when I don't.** Then I find out, or I find the
> person who knows.
> **I refer without ego.** When someone else is the right doctor for
> part of your care, you'll be in their office — with me still watching
> the whole picture.
> **Your decisions are yours.** My job is to make sure they are
> informed, unrushed, and genuinely yours.
