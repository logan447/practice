# House Style

*Standing rule (owner-approved 2026-08-21). This is the default writing
standard for every patient-facing, public-facing, marketing, outreach,
website, and practice material, unless the owner explicitly says
otherwise for a specific piece. It applies to buttons, headings, FAQs,
brochures, letters, website copy, patient documents, emails, articles,
and everything else this project creates. New drafts follow it from the
first line. When editing older material, bring it up to this standard.*

---

## 1. The standard

Write at about a 5th-grade reading level. Clear, concise, thoughtful,
direct, professional, mature, simple. Plain writing is the goal.

Two editing rules apply to every draft:

1. **If a word, sentence, or paragraph is not doing important work,
   remove it.**
2. **When two versions say the same thing, prefer the shorter and
   plainer one.**

## 2. Use

- short sentences
- short paragraphs
- common words
- active voice
- concrete statements
- clear headings that state facts, not teasers
- direct explanations
- natural human language
- enough detail to answer the question, but no more
- medical terms only when needed, with plain-language explanations
  when appropriate

## 3. Avoid

- marketing language
- sales language
- hype
- cute or clever phrasing
- slogans, unless one genuinely adds value
- AI-sounding prose
- unnecessary adjectives
- vague claims
- inflated language
- excessive reassurance
- repetition
- filler
- long introductions
- complicated sentence structures
- excessive commas
- rhetorical flourishes
- em dashes
- unnecessary metaphors
- phrases that sound polished but say very little

On em dashes: rewrite the sentence instead of punctuating around its
complexity. Use a period, a comma, or two sentences. (See §8 for how
this applies to already-approved canonical phrases.)

## 4. Explain, do not persuade

We are not trying to convince people through clever writing. We explain
what the practice does clearly enough that the right person can decide
whether it is useful to them.

Prefer:

> I offer primary care by video, home visit, and in-person visits when
> needed.

over:

> Healthcare designed around you, wherever life takes you.

The first is plain. The second sounds like advertising. If a sentence
would fit in an ad for any practice, it says nothing about this one.
Cut it or replace it with a fact.

## 5. Respect other physicians and the wider system

This binds every material, and it binds referral materials hardest.

- Never imply "your doctor does not have enough time for you, but I
  do." Not directly, and not by contrast.
- Traditional physicians work within difficult systems and often
  provide excellent care under real constraints. Those constraints are
  not marketing material. Do not use them as an attack.
- Do not disparage other physicians, traditional primary care, health
  systems, or appointment lengths to make this practice look better.
- **Say what I do. Do not say what others fail to do.** "Visits are as
  long as they need to be" is fine. "Unlike other doctors, I have time
  for you" is not.
- When writing for another physician or health professional, position
  the practice as complementary. The message is:

  > You handle what you do well. I can help with the ongoing
  > primary-care work around it.

  That work can include: ongoing primary care, home visits, lifestyle
  changes, follow-up between specialist visits, medication management,
  prevention, chronic disease management, helping patients carry out a
  treatment plan, coordination across clinicians, and primary-care
  needs outside the specialist's scope.
- In patient-facing copy, describe the patient's experience without
  assigning blame. A reader can feel unheard without us saying their
  doctors failed them.

## 6. Rules of record from this project

Accumulated owner preferences and standing decisions. These bind all
materials alongside the sections above.

1. **Credential accuracy** (design.md rule 3). The physician completed
   an internal medicine internship at George Washington University. He
   is not residency-trained and not board-certified, and no material
   may state or imply either. Lifestyle medicine certification is "in
   progress" until earned. Self-description: "physician" or "primary
   care physician," never "internist."
2. **No claims that outrun evidence** (R-13). Before real outcomes
   exist: no results, outcomes, savings, productivity, or testimonial
   claims. Write about time, attention, process, and personalization.
3. **The price is never hidden.** $100 a month appears early and
   plainly (homepage hero, brochure cover). Hiding the number is a dark
   pattern.
4. **State the limits.** Business hours. Not an emergency service. Real
   time away each year. Adults only. A panel that closes when full.
   Honesty about limits is the trust strategy, not a disclaimer.
5. **No dark patterns.** No urgency, countdowns, exit popups, hidden
   pricing, or manufactured scarcity. The panel limit is stated as a
   fact, not used as pressure.
6. **One call to action everywhere:** "Book a free conversation."
7. **First person, one person.** The physician writes as "I." Never
   "we"; there is no "we." Even the smallest page sounds like a person.
8. **No emojis, anywhere, ever.** Icons are drawn line icons or plain
   typographic marks.
9. **No invented numbers.** Every figure is sourced, derived, or
   labeled an estimate. Perishable figures carry `REFRESH-AT-RUNWAY`.
10. **Canonical phrases are reused verbatim** wherever they appear
    (see §8).
11. **No PHI** in public materials or forms. Booking collects name,
    email, phone, and referral source only.
12. **Counsel pass before publishing or printing.** All public copy
    gets the TMB-advertising review (`NEEDS-COUNSEL`).
13. **Buttons say what they do.** "Book a free conversation," not
    "Get started."
14. **Duplication rule.** The website is the deep source. Print and
    other materials summarize and route to it. Never fork detailed
    content (coverage mechanics, the agreement, the CV, FAQ) into a
    second home.

## 7. Reading level in practice

- Patient-facing materials target about 5th grade. Check drafts with a
  readability score (Flesch-Kincaid or similar) and by reading aloud.
  The score is a guide, not the goal; a plain sentence that scores 7th
  grade beats a choppy one that scores 4th.
- Clinician-facing materials stay plain in structure and sentence
  length, but clinical vocabulary is appropriate there. "Medication
  management" needs no translation for a physician.
- Legal documents (the Agreement, Practice Terms) follow their own
  drafting standards, but their patient-facing summaries follow this
  guide.

## 8. Canonical phrases and already-approved copy

Canonical phrases stay verbatim wherever they appear, even where they
conflict with §3 (several contain em dashes). Revising a canonical
phrase is an owner decision, because each one lives on multiple
surfaces:

- "away means slower — never unaware"
- "when my panel is full, it is full"
- "that is the whole catch"
- "if I'm not the right fit, I'll say so and point you toward someone
  good"
- the emergency strip, in its long and short forms

The website copy approved 2026-08-17/18 predates this guide. It stands
as approved until a dedicated revision pass. That pass should check,
at minimum:

- em dashes throughout (used heavily; §3 now says avoid)
- reading level against the 5th-grade target
- §5 physician-respect review of the time-contrast lines, especially
  homepage §2 ("no one is looking at the whole of it, with you" and
  "nobody has time to think it through with you") and homepage §3
  ("the one thing modern medicine rarely has: enough time")

Do not rewrite approved copy piecemeal; queue findings for one pass
with owner approval.

## 9. Checklist before any material is called done

1. Read it aloud. Does it sound like a person explaining, or like an ad?
2. Reading-level check run (patient-facing pieces).
3. Every claim is a fact we can stand behind; no outcomes claims (R-13).
4. Credential language exact.
5. Limits stated where enrollment is implied. Adults only where relevant.
6. Price visible if the piece discusses joining.
7. One CTA, and it is "Book a free conversation."
8. No em dashes outside canonical phrases. No emojis.
9. §5 respect check: nothing contrasts this practice against other
   clinicians' constraints.
10. Shorter, plainer version considered for every sentence.
11. Counsel pass scheduled before it goes public or to print.
