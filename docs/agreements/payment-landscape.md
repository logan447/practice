## 1. The headline findings

1. **🟥 The fee-inventory problem is the central legal constraint on any
   hybrid.** The 2004 HHS-OIG Special Fraud Alert (enforced in the Rocamora
   ~$107k and GreenField ~$287k settlements) holds that a Medicare
   participating physician's membership fee may not cover, even in part,
   Medicare-covered services — and OIG specifically treated **"extra time,"
   "coordination of care with other providers," and "a comprehensive
   assessment and plan for optimum health"** as at least partly covered.
   **That list is nearly a description of this practice's product.** The
   compliant-hybrid space for Medicare patients is therefore narrow and
   requires careful legal engineering (this is how MDVIP-style fees survive:
   the fee formally buys a non-covered wellness battery, not time or
   coordination). Consequence: for Medicare patients, "bill insurance +
   charge for the relationship layer" is mostly **not** available in the
   form we'd naturally want. (Sources: GAO-05-929; medicare.gov concierge
   page; OIG enforcement records; Wachler, Burr & Forman analyses.)

2. **🟥 No cash arrangements with Medicare patients without opting out.**
   SSA §1848(g)(4) mandates claim submission for every covered service to a
   Medicare beneficiary; opt-out (all-or-nothing, 2-year terms, private
   contracts per 42 CFR 405 Subpart D) is the only full exit. There is no
   partial opt-out — an opted-out physician cannot bill Medicare for
   *anyone*. This makes the Medicare posture decision (X-04) binary in an
   uncomfortable way for a practice serving "healthy aging" patients.

3. **🟥 Medicaid is effectively incompatible with membership fees**
   (42 CFR §447.15 payment-in-full; Texas TMHP rules; QMB dual-eligibles may
   never be balance-billed even with signed agreements). Compliant practices
   typically exclude Medicaid from membership models entirely.

4. **🟨 Commercial hybrids live or die by hold-harmless clauses.** In-network
   contracts near-universally make the negotiated rate + cost-sharing
   payment-in-full for covered services; insurers have terminated and sued
   physicians over member retainer fees. A hybrid alongside in-network
   participation requires contract-by-contract review — *never* assumed from
   "MDVIP does it."

5. **🟥 Texas affirmatively protects the DPC/periodic-fee model.** HB 1945
   (2015), Tex. Occ. Code ch. 162 subch. F: a periodic-fee medical service
   agreement is not insurance, not TDI-regulated. The pure direct-pay
   version of this practice has a clean statutory home in Texas.

6. **🟥 The Good Faith Estimate duty applies to us.** No Surprises Act (45
   CFR 149.610): every uninsured/self-pay patient who schedules care gets a
   written, itemized estimate on deadlines (1–3 business days); final bills
   exceeding the GFE by $400+ trigger a patient dispute process. **Design
   consequence: our proposal format should be built to satisfy GFE
   requirements natively** — one document, one number, kept.

7. **🟩 A new HSA fact changes pricing design at the margin:** effective
   Jan 1 2026 (OBBBA), DPC fees up to **$150/mo individual / $300 family**
   no longer break HSA eligibility and are qualified expenses — *if* the
   arrangement is solely primary care (no general anesthesia, non-vaccine
   drugs, or labs bundled). Prices near $150/mo now sit on a tax-advantaged
   cliff worth designing around. Whether our arrangements qualify:
   `NEEDS-COUNSEL` (tax).

## 2. What patients are accustomed to (familiarity findings)

- **Copay at check-in** is the most familiar payment ritual in medicine —
  small, fixed, at time of service.
- **Subscription auto-pay** is second nature from consumer life; DPC
  explicitly markets "about your cell phone bill." 🟦 Card-on-file is
  effectively standard in membership practices (MGMA: most *traditional*
  practices still don't use it; disclosure of charge triggers is the
  acceptance driver — and care may not be conditioned on providing a card).
- **The most hated experience is the surprise balance bill weeks later** —
  slow post-adjudication collection is the #1 small-practice pain point.
  Fixed, known, in-advance pricing is the antidote and is exactly what our
  proposal structure produces.
- Digital payment links are strongly preferred over paper statements.
- 🟦 No-show fees: $25–75 with 24–48h windows is normal; ethical per AMA if
  disclosed; 🟨 not chargeable to Medicaid patients; chargeable to Medicare
  patients only as a uniform, non-billed policy. (Membership models mostly
  don't need them.)
- 🟦 Superbills for out-of-network reimbursement are routine to offer,
  cost little, and reimburse the *patient*, not us; membership fees as such
  can't be superbilled — itemized services can. 🟨 Whether the patient's
  plan pays anything is their OON benefit's problem.

**Sanity read against our archetypes:** P (~$1,300/yr ≈ $108/mo) sits above
standard TX DPC ($79–99) — justified only if the delivered experience is
visibly more than DPC volume care, and note the $150/mo HSA line above it.
S–U (~$2,200–$4,700) sit inside the concierge fee band. C (~$6,400) sits
above concierge but *inside* the functional-medicine program band. E
(~$1,500/episode) has no clean comparator — nearest is 3 cash visits
($400–600) plus coordination, so its premium must be explicitly justified
(or it competes on the insurance path's failures, not its price).

## 4. Payment mechanics — the emerging default design (🟩 choices)

Pending validation and counsel, the design that best matches patient
familiarity and our proposal structure:

1. **Monthly card-on-file auto-pay as the default** for ongoing
   arrangements (quarterly/annual prepay optional, possibly with a modest
   prepay discount — 🟦 in DPC). Clear written disclosure of what triggers
   any charge; care never conditioned on card-on-file.
2. **Episodes (E) and front-loaded engagements (C):** staged payments —
   initial payment at start, then monthly — rather than all-upfront;
   proposal doubles as GFE.
3. **No no-show fees** in membership arrangements (visits are prepaid
   attention); a standard disclosed policy only for any à-la-carte work.
4. **Superbills offered** on request for itemizable services; zero promises
   about OON reimbursement.
5. **Travel:** included in home-visit arrangement prices (C archetype
   pattern) rather than surcharged — simpler, and the framework already
   counts travel time as attributable hours. Revisit if geography scatters.
6. **Refund/proration terms** stated plainly in every proposal
   (`NEEDS-COUNSEL` for Texas consumer-protection drafting).

## 5. What this does to the payer-strategy question (X-04)

The research reframes the options more sharply than before:

- **Path A — Direct-pay practice (Texas DPC statutory home).** Cleanest
  legal position; full pricing freedom; our whole value proposition
  (time, coordination, whole-picture planning) is sellable without
  fee-inventory gymnastics. Cost: patient affordability carried entirely
  out-of-pocket; Medicare patients require opt-out (or exclusion) —
  painful for the healthy-aging mission.
- **Path B — In-network hybrid.** Insurance absorbs visit costs (patient
  copays), private fee for the rest — but the fee inventory must exclude
  everything covered, which is *exactly what we sell*; plus hold-harmless
  contract review per payer, claims/coding/audit apparatus (False Claims
  Act exposure, documentation segregation), and the Q-02 admin burden.
  Viable, heavily lawyered, and it constrains the product's soul.
- **Path C — Out-of-network + superbills.** Middle path: direct-pay
  simplicity, patients with PPO OON benefits recover something, no network
  contracts to violate. Medicare rules still apply to Medicare patients
  (mandatory claims / opt-out logic unchanged).
- **Mixed panels are possible** (DPC members + insured non-members;
  🟨 contract-dependent) but multiply admin complexity (R-20/R-22).

**No decision is made here.** But the research shifts the prior: the
practice's specific value proposition fits Path A/C far more naturally than
Path B, and fits Medicare participation awkwardly in all forms. The
counterweight remains affordability (S-archetype patients) and the Medicare-
age mission. → X-04, decided with counsel, informed by Q-02 practical
learning.

## 6. Questions only a healthcare attorney can answer (carry to counsel)

1. Fee-inventory legality per item against current CMS coverage (AWV, CCM,
   TCM, e-visit codes have eaten the historic "non-covered" space); whether
   to seek an OIG advisory opinion.
2. What our actual future payer contracts permit (hold-harmless, access-fee,
   all-products, notice clauses) — including Medicare Advantage plans,
   which follow their own rules and were not analyzed.
3. Participating vs non-participating vs opt-out election mechanics and
   calendar, given intended patient mix.
4. Intake screening so no QMB/Medicaid patient is ever offered/charged a fee.
5. Whether hybrid billing takes an agreement outside the Texas DPC safe
   harbor (Occ. Code ch. 162 subch. F drafting).
6. HSA qualification of our arrangements post-OBBBA ($150/$300 caps,
   "solely primary care" limits) — tax counsel.
7. GFE compliance mechanics for proposal-based pricing; refund/proration
   and Texas DTPA exposure; TMB non-abandonment obligations at
   non-renewal.

## 7. Honest gaps

No reliable statistics on card-on-file adoption, deposit prevalence, or
superbill uptake; DPC price data converges but isn't a census; MDVIP's exact
fees are location-opaque; whether a DPC membership fee itself belongs in a
GFE is unresolved in CMS guidance; AWV exact dollar amounts conflicted
across sources by ~$20; all functional-medicine pricing is self-published
marketing. None of these gaps blocks design; all are flagged where used.
